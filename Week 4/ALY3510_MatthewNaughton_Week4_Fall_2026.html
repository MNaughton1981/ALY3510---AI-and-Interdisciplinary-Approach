Teaching a Machine to See:
Learning From Data With Google's Teachable Machine

Machine learning is often described in abstract terms, yet the core idea is intuitive: a system improves at a task by being exposed to examples rather than by following explicitly programmed rules (Russell & Norvig, 2021). Google's Teachable Machine makes this idea tangible by letting anyone train an image, audio, or pose classifier directly in a web browser, with no code required (Carney et al., 2020). For this exercise, a two-class image classifier was trained on webcam selfies of myself. As the analysis will show, the way I defined those two classes turned the project into an unexpectedly clear demonstration of how a model learns the statistics of the images it is given rather than the idea the designer had in mind. This essay documents how the model learned from that data, what was observed during training and testing, and how the machine's learning process resembles and diverges from human learning. The central argument is that Teachable Machine is a faithful small-scale demonstration of statistical pattern learning, but that this very fidelity exposes the deep gap between correlation-based machine learning and the conceptual, data-efficient learning characteristic of people.

How the Model Learned From Data

Teachable Machine is built on TensorFlow.js and runs entirely in the browser (Carney et al., 2020). For image projects it does not train a neural network from scratch. Instead it uses a technique called transfer learning on top of MobileNet, a convolutional neural network that was previously trained on the large ImageNet dataset to recognize general visual features such as edges, textures, and shapes (Howard et al., 2017). The pre-trained portion of the network is frozen and used as a fixed feature extractor: each uploaded image is converted into a compact numerical vector, or embedding, that summarizes its visual content. Only a small classifier layer on top is actually trained on the user's examples. This is why a usable model can be produced in seconds from a few dozen images rather than requiring thousands of samples and hours of computation.

The training itself follows the standard supervised-learning loop. Each sample carries a label (the class the user assigned it to), and the model repeatedly adjusts the weights of its final layer to reduce the gap between its predictions and those labels. This gap is measured by a loss function (categorical cross-entropy), and the weights are updated through backpropagation and gradient descent across several passes over the data called epochs (Goodfellow et al., 2016). Teachable Machine exposes a few of these controls under advanced settings — epochs, batch size, and learning rate — which lets a learner see directly how optimization hyperparameters influence the result. What Was Observed

Experimental Setup

An Image Project was created with two classes of webcam selfies, captured at my desk under ordinary indoor lighting. I intended the two classes to capture two different ideas: Class 1 (15 image samples) was meant to represent my facial expressions and consisted of straight-on, forward-facing shots in which I varied my expression; Class 2 (20 image samples) was meant to represent different camera perspectives and consisted of shots taken from varied head angles — profile, looking up and down, and turning left and right, often with a hand resting near my chin. In total only 35 images were used. The model was trained with the default settings and tested in the live preview window using poses it had not seen during capture. As the observations below explain, defining the two classes along different conceptual dimensions — expression versus viewpoint — rather than as two parallel categories proved to be the most revealing part of the experiment.

Observations During Training and Testing

My first observation was simply how quickly and confidently the model trained. Despite a tiny dataset of only 35 images, training completed in a few seconds and the live preview produced decisive predictions — a direct consequence of the pre-trained MobileNet features doing most of the work. When I sat straight on and looked directly into the webcam, the model classified me as Class 1 with about 90% confidence, leaving roughly 10% for Class 2 (see Figure 4). When I instead turned my head into a profile, looked up or down, or raised a hand toward my chin, the prediction swung toward Class 2.

The most instructive observation, however, was about what the model actually learned. I had intended Class 1 to represent facial expressions and Class 2 to represent camera perspectives — two conceptually different ideas. But the model has no access to my intent; it only sees two sets of images and searches for the pixel pattern that best separates them. Because my Class 1 samples were almost all forward-facing while my Class 2 samples showed turned heads and angled views (often with a hand near my face), the feature that most reliably told the two sets apart was head orientation and hand presence, not expression versus viewpoint. This explained the behavior I saw: changing my expression while facing forward barely moved the prediction, whereas changing only my head angle flipped it. The model had learned the surface cue that happened to divide my two folders of images, not the abstract distinction I had in mind.

Two further observations followed from this. First, the dataset was both small and imbalanced — 15 versus 20 images — yet the model still produced confident outputs, a useful warning that a model can look convincing while having learned the wrong thing. Second, and more fundamentally, this is a textbook case of a model optimizing for whatever statistical signal most reliably separates the classes in the training data rather than for the concept the human intended (Lake et al., 2017). A cleaner experiment would isolate a single dimension — for instance, holding my head angle fixed and varying only my expression — so that each label corresponds to one well-defined distinction. The flawed design was, in the end, more illuminating than a tidy result would have been, because it exposed the gap between my intention and the model's actual learning.

Machine Learning Versus Human Learning

Similarities

Several parallels between the model's behavior and human learning are striking. Both learn inductively from examples rather than from exhaustive rules, and both improve as the quantity and variety of those examples increase. Both also benefit from prior knowledge: just as the model reused MobileNet's general visual features to learn a usable classifier from only 35 images, a person draws on a lifetime of prior visual experience when learning to recognize something new. Finally, both are prone to seizing on misleading shortcuts in biased examples — the model's reliance on head orientation parallels the way a young child, shown a narrow set of examples, may briefly treat an incidental feature as the defining one before refining the concept (Lake et al., 2017).

Differences

The differences, however, are more fundamental. Table 1 contrasts the two along several dimensions. In brief, humans are extraordinarily data-efficient, often learning a new category from one or two examples, whereas even a transfer-learning model benefits from many samples. Humans learn causal and semantic structure and can explain their reasoning, while the model learns only statistical correlations between pixels and labels and has no understanding of what it is classifying (Lake et al., 2017; Russell & Norvig, 2021). Human learning is continual, embodied, and multimodal, whereas the model's learning is a discrete batch optimization that is frozen once training stops and is subject to catastrophic forgetting if retrained carelessly (Goodfellow et al., 2016).

Conclusions

Training a classifier in Teachable Machine demonstrated, in a few minutes and without code, the essential mechanics of supervised machine learning: labeled examples are converted into numerical features, and a model iteratively adjusts its parameters to minimize prediction error. The exercise made several abstract ideas concrete — transfer learning, the value of varied training data, confidence scores, and the risk of learning spurious correlations instead of the intended concept. At the same time, observing the model's limitations clarified how different machine learning is from human cognition. The model is a powerful pattern matcher that generalizes from statistics, but it does not understand, reason, or learn with the efficiency and flexibility of a person. For an analytics practitioner, the practical lesson is twofold: curating representative, unbiased training data is as important as any algorithmic choice, and model outputs should be interpreted as calibrated correlations rather than as human-like judgments. Training on my own selfies made this concrete: although I intended one class to represent facial expressions and the other to represent camera perspectives, the model simply learned the visual feature — head orientation — that happened to separate the two sets of images, classifying a forward-facing selfie as Class 1 with roughly 90% confidence regardless of my expression. No human observer would confuse "what expression is this?" with "which way is the head turned?", yet the model had no way to know which question I was asking. As I move further into analytical decision making, this exercise reinforces that the credibility of any model I build rests first on how carefully I define my classes and curate representative data.
